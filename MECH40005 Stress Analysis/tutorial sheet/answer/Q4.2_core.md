# Q4.2 Solution core: volume change from internal pressure + heating (thin-walled cylinder / thin-walled spherical tank)

> **One-line conclusion**: (a) internal volume change of the cylinder $\Delta V_{\text{cyl}}=0.101\ \text{m}^3$ (≈101 L); (b) spherical tank of the same radius and thickness $\Delta V_{\text{sph}}=0.100\ \text{m}^3$ (≈100 L).
>
> **Note on the question title (must read)**: the team task title says "thick-walled cylinder", but the data given in the original question are $R_m=2\ \text{m}$, $t=120\ \text{mm}$, so $R_m/t=16.7$, and the question itself **explicitly asks for a thin-walled (membrane) solution** - it gives no inner or outer radius and requests no Lamé solution. This note stays faithful to the original question: the main solution is the thin-walled membrane solution; the thick-walled Lamé solution appears only in §7 as a check on the "order of magnitude of the thin-wall error" (the result differs by $-4.7\%$, mainly from the reference volume rather than from the stress) and **is not the answer**. No problem was substituted (Q4.3 / Q4.5 were not brought in), because Q4.2 has complete data and definite answers to both parts, so no replacement is needed.
>

**Find**

## 3. Assumptions and failure criteria

**Mechanical assumptions (thin-wall)**

1. **Thin-walled membrane assumption**: $R/t=16.7\gg1$ (engineering practice accepts $\gtrsim10$), the stress is uniform through the wall, and the mid-surface radius $R_m$ replaces the inner radius ($R_i\approx R_m$).
2. **Plane stress**: the radial stress $\sigma_r$ runs from $-p$ at the inner surface to $0$ at the outer surface, so its magnitude is $\le p$, a factor $R/t\approx16.7$ smaller than $\sigma_\theta$; it is neglected. The **radial strain, however, cannot be neglected** (it arises from the Poisson effect).
3. **Closed ends**: the end caps pass the axial resultant of the internal pressure into the wall, so $\sigma_z$ exists. For an open tube or a free end (a pipe, say), $\sigma_z=0$ and the result differs.
4. **Small deformation, linear elastic, isotropic**: superposition holds, so the pressure term and the temperature term can be added directly; $E,\nu,\alpha$ are independent of temperature and stress.
5. **Uniform temperature field**: the whole wall rises by $\Delta T=25\ \text{K}$ at once, with no gradient ⇒ no thermal stress (free expansion); the temperature only contributes the **free thermal strain** $\alpha\Delta T$. If there were a gradient through the wall (transient), a self-equilibrating thermal stress would arise and this solution would not apply.
6. **No external constraint**: the vessel can expand freely and the shell carries no external support reaction.
7. **$p$ is gauge pressure**, the outside is atmospheric, and the pressure difference across the wall is $p$.

**Failure criteria (not checked here, listed as design background)**

- The yield strength of aluminium is about $\sigma_Y\approx 100\text{–}270\ \text{MPa}$ (depending on grade and heat treatment); here $\sigma_\theta=83\ \text{MPa}$, so $\sigma_\theta/\sigma_Y$ is already $0.3\text{–}0.8$ - this is **not** a case with "a large safety margin", it is just a teaching example.
- Compare with yield using **von Mises / Tresca** (thin-walled vessel: $\sigma_1=\sigma_\theta,\ \sigma_2=\sigma_z,\ \sigma_3\approx0$):
  - Tresca: $\sigma_\theta-\sigma_r\approx\sigma_\theta=83.3\ \text{MPa}$;
  - von Mises: $\sqrt{\sigma_\theta^2-\sigma_\theta\sigma_z+\sigma_z^2}=\sqrt{83.3^2-83.3\times41.7+41.7^2}=72.2\ \text{MPa}$.
- The temperature term produces no stress (free expansion), so it **does not enter the strength check**, only the volume change.
- This question asks about **deformation / volume**, not strength, so the failure criteria are used only to judge whether the result is meaningful in engineering terms.

## 4. Governing equations and boundary conditions

### 4.1 Equilibrium (membrane, axisymmetric)

**Hoop stress in the cylinder**: cut the cylinder in half along its axis and take the half as a free body; the resultant of the internal pressure on the projected area $2RL$ is balanced by $\sigma_\theta$ on the two wall sections:

$$
2\,\sigma_\theta\,t\,L=p\,(2R)\,L
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\boxed{\sigma_\theta=\frac{pR}{t}}
$$

**Axial stress in the cylinder**: cut once around the circumference; the pressure $p\,\pi R^2$ on the end cap is balanced by $\sigma_z$ on the wall ring area $2\pi Rt$:

$$
\sigma_z\,(2\pi R t)=p\,(\pi R^{2})
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\boxed{\sigma_z=\frac{pR}{2t}=\frac{\sigma_\theta}{2}}
$$

**Stress in the sphere** (spherically symmetric, the two principal stresses are equal): cut a hemisphere,

$$
\sigma\,(2\pi R t)=p\,(\pi R^{2})
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\boxed{\sigma_\phi=\sigma_\theta=\frac{pR}{2t}}
$$

> The denominator for the sphere is $2t$, not $t$ - this is the most common place to lose marks in this problem.

### 4.2 Constitutive (3D Hooke's law + thermal strain)

$$
\varepsilon_\theta=\frac{1}{E}\big[\sigma_\theta-\nu(\sigma_r+\sigma_z)\big]+\alpha\Delta T,
\qquad
\varepsilon_z=\frac{1}{E}\big[\sigma_z-\nu(\sigma_\theta+\sigma_r)\big]+\alpha\Delta T
$$

$$
\varepsilon_r=\frac{1}{E}\big[\sigma_r-\nu(\sigma_\theta+\sigma_z)\big]+\alpha\Delta T
$$

Sphere (all three directions on the same footing):

$$
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{E}\big[\sigma-\nu(\sigma+\sigma_r)\big]+\alpha\Delta T
\;\xrightarrow{\sigma_r\approx0}\;
\varepsilon=\frac{\sigma(1-\nu)}{E}+\alpha\Delta T
$$

### 4.3 Geometry (strain ↔ change of size)

$$
\varepsilon_\theta=\frac{\Delta R}{R},\qquad
\varepsilon_z=\frac{\Delta L}{L},\qquad
\varepsilon_r=\frac{\Delta t}{t}\ (\text{does not enter the volume formula})
$$

### 4.4 Volume change (total differential ⇒ volumetric strain)

Cylinder $V=\pi R^2L$: $\delta V=\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial R}\delta R+\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial L}\delta L=2\pi RL\,\delta R+\pi R^2\,\delta L$; divide both sides by $V$:

$$
\boxed{\varepsilon_v=\frac{\Delta V}{V}=2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z}
$$

Sphere $V=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3$: $\delta V=4\pi R^2\delta R$; divide both sides by $V$:

$$
\boxed{\varepsilon_v=\frac{\Delta V}{V}=3\varepsilon}
$$

(Thermal expansion can be checked from this as well: $R$ and $L$ each expand by $\alpha\Delta T$ ⇒ cylinder $2\alpha\Delta T+\alpha\Delta T=3\alpha\Delta T$, sphere $3\alpha\Delta T$, the same for both - a very good self-check.)

### 4.5 Summary of boundary conditions

| Location | Condition | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| Inner surface $r=R_i$ | $\sigma_r=-p$ | Sets the magnitude of $\sigma_\theta,\sigma_z$; neglected in the thin-wall treatment |
| Outer surface $r=R_o$ | $\sigma_r=0$ | As above |
| End cap | Axial force balance $p\pi R^2=\sigma_z2\pi Rt$ | Gives $\sigma_z=pR/2t$ (closed ends) |
| Open / free end | $\sigma_z=0$ | Not used here; if open, $\Delta V_p$ would be about 25% smaller |
| Wall | No external pressure, no external constraint | Free thermal expansion ⇒ no thermal stress |
| Plane of symmetry | Sphere $\sigma_\phi=\sigma_\theta$ | Gives the equal biaxial stress in the sphere |

## 5. Step-by-step substitution (every step with its arithmetic)

### (a) Cylinder

**Step A1 Volume** ($R=2,\ L=2$)

$$
V=\pi R^{2}L=\pi\times 4\times 2=8\pi=25.133\ \text{m}^3
$$

**Step A2 The two membrane stresses** ($p=5\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}$, $R=2\ \text{m}$, $t=0.12\ \text{m}$)

$$
\sigma_\theta=\frac{pR}{t}=\frac{5\times10^{6}\times2}{0.12}=8.333\times10^{7}\ \text{Pa}=83.33\ \text{MPa}
$$

$$
\sigma_z=\frac{\sigma_\theta}{2}=4.167\times10^{7}\ \text{Pa}=41.67\ \text{MPa}
$$

**Step A3 Strains from pressure** (all in MPa, $E=69\,000\ \text{MPa}$, $\sigma_r\approx0$)

$$
\varepsilon_\theta^{p}=\frac{\sigma_\theta-\nu\sigma_z}{E}
=\frac{83.33-0.3\times41.67}{69\,000}
=\frac{83.33-12.50}{69\,000}
=\frac{70.83}{69\,000}
=1.027\times10^{-3}
$$

$$
\varepsilon_z^{p}=\frac{\sigma_z-\nu\sigma_\theta}{E}
=\frac{41.67-0.3\times83.33}{69\,000}
=\frac{41.67-25.00}{69\,000}
=\frac{16.67}{69\,000}
=2.415\times10^{-4}
$$

> The axial strain is **positive** (tension), but the Poisson term squeezes it to roughly $1/4$ of the hoop strain - do not write it as $\sigma_z/E=6.04\times10^{-4}$.

**Step A4 Volumetric strain and volume change from pressure**

$$
\varepsilon_v^{p}=2\varepsilon_\theta^{p}+\varepsilon_z^{p}
=2(1.027\times10^{-3})+2.415\times10^{-4}
=2.295\times10^{-3}
$$

$$
\Delta V_p=V\,\varepsilon_v^{p}=25.133\times2.295\times10^{-3}=5.767\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}^3
$$

**Step A5 Volumetric strain and volume change from temperature** (all three directions expand ⇒ volumetric thermal strain $3\alpha\Delta T$)

$$
3\alpha\Delta T=3\times23\times10^{-6}\times25=1.725\times10^{-3}
$$

$$
\Delta V_T=V\times3\alpha\Delta T=25.133\times1.725\times10^{-3}=4.335\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}^3
$$

**Step A6 Superpose**

$$
\Delta V_{\text{cyl}}=\Delta V_p+\Delta V_T
=5.767\times10^{-2}+4.335\times10^{-2}
=1.010\times10^{-1}\ \text{m}^3
$$

$$
\boxed{\Delta V_{\text{cyl}}=0.101\ \text{m}^3\ (\approx 101\ \text{L})}
$$

### (b) Sphere

**Step B1 Volume** (same $R=2$)

$$
V_s=\frac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}=\frac{4}{3}\pi\times8=33.510\ \text{m}^3
$$

**Step B2 Stress in the sphere** (both principal stresses equal this)

$$
\sigma=\frac{pR}{2t}=\frac{5\times10^{6}\times2}{2\times0.12}=4.167\times10^{7}\ \text{Pa}=41.67\ \text{MPa}
$$

**Step B3 Strain** ($\sigma_r\approx0$, equal biaxial ⇒ Poisson coefficient $(1+\nu)$ leads to the result $(1-\nu)$)

$$
\varepsilon=\frac{\sigma(1-\nu)}{E}
=\frac{41.67\times(1-0.3)}{69\,000}
=\frac{29.17}{69\,000}
=4.227\times10^{-4}
$$

**Step B4 Volume change from pressure** (spherically symmetric, the same strain in three directions ⇒ $\varepsilon_v=3\varepsilon$)

$$
\varepsilon_v^{p}=3\varepsilon=3\times4.227\times10^{-4}=1.268\times10^{-3}
$$

$$
\Delta V_{p,s}=V_s\,\varepsilon_v^{p}=33.510\times1.268\times10^{-3}=4.249\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}^3
$$

**Step B5 Volume change from temperature** (again $3\alpha\Delta T$, but $V_s$ is larger)

$$
\Delta V_{T,s}=33.510\times1.725\times10^{-3}=5.781\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}^3
$$

**Step B6 Superpose**

$$
\Delta V_{\text{sph}}=4.249\times10^{-2}+5.781\times10^{-2}=1.003\times10^{-1}\ \text{m}^3
$$

$$
\boxed{\Delta V_{\text{sph}}=0.100\ \text{m}^3\ (\approx 100.3\ \text{L})}
$$

### Shortcut recomputation (also usable as a check)

The pressure term of the cylinder can be collapsed into a single expression by eliminating $\varepsilon_\theta^{p},\varepsilon_z^{p}$:

$$
2\varepsilon_\theta^{p}+\varepsilon_z^{p}=\frac{pR}{tE}\left(\frac{5}{2}-2\nu\right)
=\frac{5\times10^{6}\times2}{0.12\times69\times10^{9}}\times(2.5-0.6)
=1.2077\times10^{-3}\times1.9=2.295\times10^{-3}\ \checkmark
$$

For the sphere likewise: $\varepsilon_v^{p}=3\varepsilon=3\cdot\dfrac{pR}{2tE}(1-\nu)=\dfrac{3(1-\nu)pR}{2tE}=1.268\times10^{-3}\ \checkmark$

**(2) Magnitude / plausibility**

- Total volumetric strain $\varepsilon_v=4.0\times10^{-3}$: the elastic strain of aluminium runs up to about $\sigma_Y/E\approx0.1\text{–}0.4\%$, plus a thermal strain $\alpha\Delta T\cdot3\approx0.17\%$, giving $0.4\%$ in total - **the same order as $10^{-3}$, plausible**; a result of $10^{-1}$ or $10^{-6}$ would be wrong.
- $\Delta V$ must be of order $V\cdot10^{-3}=\mathcal O(10^{-2})\ \text{m}^3$; here it is $0.1\ \text{m}^3$ ✓.
- The size changes themselves are small: $\Delta R=R\varepsilon_\theta=2\times1.03\times10^{-3}=2.1\ \text{mm}$, $\Delta L=L\varepsilon_z=0.48\ \text{mm}$ - the small-deformation assumption ($\ll R$) holds ✓.
- Ratio of the two contributions: cylinder pressure/temperature $=5.77/4.34=1.33$; sphere $=4.25/5.78=0.74$. Neither term dominates ⇒ **you cannot compute only one of them**.

**(5) Order of magnitude of the thin-wall error (Lamé thick-walled comparison, not the answer)**

The question gives the mid-surface radius $R_m=2$; if you take the thick-walled geometry strictly as $R_i=1.94$, $R_o=2.06$ and recheck the internal volume change with the Lamé solution:

$$
\sigma_\theta(r)=\frac{pa^2}{b^2-a^2}\left(1+\frac{b^2}{r^2}\right),\quad
\sigma_r(r)=\frac{pa^2}{b^2-a^2}\left(1-\frac{b^2}{r^2}\right),\quad
\sigma_z=\frac{pa^2}{b^2-a^2}
$$

At $r=a=1.94$: $\sigma_\theta=83.4\ \text{MPa}$ (thin-wall $83.3$, a difference of $0.1\%$), $\sigma_r=-5.0\ \text{MPa}=-p$ ✓ (the boundary condition is consistent), $\sigma_z=39.2\ \text{MPa}$ (thin-wall $41.7$, a difference of $-6\%$); hence $\varepsilon_\theta=1.06\times10^{-3}$, $\varepsilon_z=2.27\times10^{-4}$, $\varepsilon_v^{p}=2.35\times10^{-3}$, and with the bore volume $V=\pi a^2L=23.65\ \text{m}^3$ the result is $\Delta V=0.096\ \text{m}^3$, which is **$4.7\%$ lower** than the thin-wall answer.

**The error comes mainly from the "reference volume"** ($23.65$ vs $25.13\ \text{m}^3$, $-5.9\%$), not from the stress (the strain terms differ by only $+2.3\%$). In other words: **as long as the question prescribes $R_m$ as the characteristic radius, the thin-wall solution is the answer the question wants**; switching to Lamé would not be "more correct", because the reference volume changes too. This comparison shows only that with $R_m/t=16.7$ the thin-wall solution is in error by of order $5\%$, which is acceptable for engineering estimates.
