# Q4.2 Plain-language walkthrough: inflate it and heat it - how much more can the tank hold?

> This is the plain-language version of [Q4.2_core.md](Q4.2_core.md): its only job is to turn the derivation into everyday language, and **every number and conclusion follows core - not a single figure is changed here**. The original question is [Q4.2-cylinder-pressure-thermal-volume.md](../Q4.2-cylinder-pressure-thermal-volume.md).
>
> The conclusion first: **(a) cylinder $\Delta V\approx0.101\ \text{m}^3\approx101\ \text{L}$; (b) spherical tank of the same radius and thickness $\Delta V\approx0.100\ \text{m}^3\approx100\ \text{L}$.**

> On the title: the task is called "thick-walled cylinder", but the data give $R_m/t=16.7$ and the original question explicitly asks for a thin-walled (membrane) solution - core states this in its first paragraph, and the Lamé solution appears only as an error check in §7 (differing by $-4.7\%$, mainly from the reference volume). This note follows the thin-wall main solution and does not substitute a different problem.

---

## 1. The one-line intuition

Two things stretch the tank at once: **the internal pressure pushes from inside** (stress → strain), and **heating makes the material itself longer** (free thermal strain, which produces no stress). Volume goes as the cube of a length, so

$$
\Delta V \approx \varepsilon_v \times V,\qquad
\text{cylinder: }\varepsilon_v=2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z,\qquad
\text{sphere: }\varepsilon_v=3\varepsilon
$$

The pressure term is larger for the cylinder (its hoop stress is twice that of the sphere), the temperature term is larger for the spherical tank (at the same radius the sphere holds $33.5>25.1\ \text{m}^3$), and the two offset each other - $101\ \text{L}$ against $100\ \text{L}$, almost level. It looks like a coincidence, but it is bound to happen because each vessel wins one of the two terms.

---

## 2. Step-by-step: what each step computes and why

### Step 0 First be clear about what is being computed

**What**: the change of the **internal volume** that holds the fluid - $V=\pi R^2L$ (cylinder), $V=\tfrac43\pi R^3$ (sphere) - **not the volume of the wall material itself**.

**Why**: the question asks "how much more can it hold". How the wall thickness changes ($\varepsilon_r$) does not change that answer; it is part of the background. Also, because the deformation is small, we do not really compute "new size − old size", we compute a ratio $\Delta V/V=\varepsilon_v$ and multiply back at the end. That is exactly what the total-differential step does: in $V(R,L)$, $R$ and $L$ each move a little and the volume follows.

### Step 1 Compute the original volume $V$

**What**: $V=\pi\times2^2\times2=8\pi=25.133\ \text{m}^3$ (cylinder); $V_s=\tfrac43\pi\times2^3=33.510\ \text{m}^3$ (sphere).

**Why first**: it is needed in two places - (i) the final $\Delta V=V\cdot\varepsilon_v$ multiplies by it; (ii) it is the anchor for the magnitude check (the answer must be of order $V\times10^{-3}$, see core §7).

### Step 2 Compute the stresses (force balance, not Hooke's law)

**What**: cut the vessel and take half of it as a free body, then set the resultant of the internal pressure equal to the resultant of the stress in the wall.

- **Hoop** (cut along the axis): the force on the projected area $2RL$ is carried by the two wall sections $tL$ → $\sigma_\theta=\dfrac{pR}{t}=83.33\ \text{MPa}$.
- **Axial** (cut once across the axis): the pressure $p\pi R^2$ on the end cap is carried by the wall ring $2\pi Rt$ → $\sigma_z=\dfrac{pR}{2t}=41.67\ \text{MPa}=\sigma_\theta/2$.
- **Sphere** (cut a hemisphere): the two directions share it symmetrically → $\sigma=\dfrac{pR}{2t}=41.67\ \text{MPa}$.

**Why $E$ does not appear here at all**: equilibrium only asks where the force comes from and where it goes, and is independent of how stiff the material is. $E$ only appears in the next step, when the deformation is computed.

**Why the denominator for the sphere is $2t$**: on a sphere each direction has only half the "grip" available (the two directions share equally), unlike the hoop direction of a cylinder, which carries it alone. This is the first big place to lose marks in this problem.

### Step 3 Compute the strains (Hooke + Poisson + heat)

**What**: the true strain in each direction. The three normal stresses pull on each other - that is the Poisson term $-\nu(\text{the other two stresses})$:

$$
\varepsilon_\theta=\frac{\sigma_\theta-\nu\sigma_z}{E},\qquad
\varepsilon_z=\frac{\sigma_z-\nu\sigma_\theta}{E},\qquad
\text{sphere (equal biaxial) }\varepsilon=\frac{\sigma(1-\nu)}{E}
$$

and then add the thermal strain $\alpha\Delta T$ to all of them.

**Why you cannot just divide $\sigma/E$ and stop**: when the hoop direction is stretched, the Poisson effect wants to shorten the axial direction - the presence of $\sigma_z$ squeezes $\varepsilon_z$ from $6.04\times10^{-4}$ down to $2.415\times10^{-4}$ (about 1/4). Miss this term and the axial strain comes out too big.

**Why the thermal strain produces no stress**: the whole wall heats uniformly and nothing constrains it, so the material grows as much as it wants - free expansion leaves no internal force behind, only the deformation $\alpha\Delta T$. Thermal stress appears only when there is a temperature gradient, or when the part is clamped so that it cannot expand.

**The numbers here**: $\varepsilon_\theta^p=1.027\times10^{-3}$, $\varepsilon_z^p=2.415\times10^{-4}$, sphere $\varepsilon=4.227\times10^{-4}$; $\alpha\Delta T=23\times10^{-6}\times25=5.75\times10^{-4}$.

### Step 4 Turn linear strains into volumetric strain (the key move of this problem)

**What**: $V=\pi R^2L$. When $R$ moves by $\delta R$ the volume moves by $2\pi RL\,\delta R$ - **the squared term is counted twice**; when $L$ moves by $\delta L$ the volume moves by $\pi R^2\,\delta L$ - counted once. Divide both sides by $V$:

$$
\frac{\Delta V}{V}=2\frac{\Delta R}{R}+\frac{\Delta L}{L}=2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z
$$

For the sphere $V=\tfrac43\pi R^3$, a cube, so $\varepsilon_v=3\varepsilon$.

**Why this step is worth memorising**: it is simply "whatever the exponent is, that is the multiplier". The $R$ in the cylinder formula is squared, so it contributes $2\varepsilon_\theta$; many people lose marks by dropping that 2.

**A handy check**: for pure thermal expansion $\varepsilon_v=2\alpha\Delta T+\alpha\Delta T=3\alpha\Delta T$, and the sphere also gives $3\alpha\Delta T$ - two routes land on the same $1.725\times10^{-3}$, so the formula was not miscopied.

### Step 5 Compute the pressure term and the temperature term separately, then add

**What**:

$$
\Delta V_p=V\cdot(2\varepsilon_\theta^p+\varepsilon_z^p),\qquad
\Delta V_T=V\cdot3\alpha\Delta T,\qquad
\Delta V=\Delta V_p+\Delta V_T
$$

**Why adding is safe**: linear elastic + small deformation → superposition holds; and the temperature term and the pressure term are independent (one has stress, one does not), so they do not contaminate each other. Cylinder: $5.767\times10^{-2}+4.335\times10^{-2}=0.101\ \text{m}^3$.

### Step 6 Do the sphere the same way, then look back and compare

The sphere follows exactly the same route, with three substitutions: stress $pR/2t$, strain $\sigma(1-\nu)/E$, volume $3\varepsilon$. The result is $\Delta V_{\text{sph}}=0.100\ \text{m}^3$.

**How to understand why the two parts come out almost equal**: the cylinder has the higher stress ($\sigma_\theta$ is twice that of the sphere) but the smaller volume; the sphere has the lower stress but the larger volume. The accounting in core §6: the pressure term is 36% larger for the cylinder, the temperature term is 33% larger for the sphere, and after adding they are level. **Neither term can be dropped** - computing only the pressure term or only the temperature term puts the answer more than 30% out.

### The 30-second exam flow chart

$$
V \;\to\; \sigma\ (pR/t,\ pR/2t) \;\to\; \varepsilon\ (\text{with Poisson}+\alpha\Delta T) \;\to\; \varepsilon_v\ (2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z\ /\ 3\varepsilon) \;\to\; \Delta V_p+\Delta V_T
$$

## 3. Common mistakes (many people ... whereas in fact ...)

1. **Many people** write the volumetric strain of the cylinder as $\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z$; **in fact** the radius is squared in $V=\pi R^2L$, so a change in radius counts twice - the right form is $2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z$. Drop that 2 and the pressure term is a third too small.
2. **Many people** think heating creates a thermal stress that stretches the tank further; **in fact** with uniform heating and no constraint the thermal strain produces no stress at all, and the temperature term is simply $3\alpha\Delta T$. A thermal stress needs a temperature gradient (transient) or an external constraint, and this problem has neither.
3. **Many people** memorise the sphere stress as $pR/t$ (confusing it with the cylinder); **in fact** for a sphere it is $pR/(2t)$ - the two directions share equally. Make that mistake and the sphere strain and $\Delta V$ are both out by a factor of two.
4. **Many people** write $\varepsilon_z=\sigma_z/E$ for the axial strain; **in fact** with three normal stresses the Poisson term must be there, $\varepsilon_z=(\sigma_z-\nu\sigma_\theta)/E$ - the hoop tension pinches the axial direction back, giving $2.415\times10^{-4}$ rather than $6.04\times10^{-4}$.
5. **Many people** read "thin wall, neglect $\sigma_r$" as "the radial direction can be ignored completely"; **in fact** only the stress $\sigma_r$ is neglected (it is $R/t\approx16.7$ times smaller than $\sigma_\theta$); the radial **strain** $\varepsilon_r$ is still produced by the Poisson effect - it only changes the wall thickness, so it never appears in the internal-volume formula.
6. **Many people** carry mixed units, putting $t=120$ and $E=69$ straight into the formula; **in fact** either go fully SI ($t=0.12\ \text{m}$, $E=69\times10^9\ \text{Pa}$) or fully MPa ($E=69\,000\ \text{MPa}$) - both ends consistent, otherwise you are out by six orders of magnitude.

## 4. Memory cards

**Thin-cylinder stress** | Front: what are the hoop and axial stresses in a closed thin-walled cylinder, and why do they differ by a factor of two? | Back: $\sigma_\theta=pR/t$, $\sigma_z=pR/2t=\sigma_\theta/2$. The hoop direction carries the whole projected pressure on its own, while the axial direction has the end caps passing the force but a larger wall ring area - cut a free body and it falls out, no need to memorise it.

**Sphere stress** | Front: what is the stress formula for a thin spherical shell? | Back: $\sigma=\sigma_\phi=\sigma_\theta=\dfrac{pR}{2t}$, the two principal stresses are equal. The denominator is $2t$ - spherical symmetry, two directions sharing equally; this is the only, but fatal, difference from the cylinder.

**Volumetric strain** | Front: how do you write $\Delta V/V$ for a cylinder and a sphere in terms of strain? | Back: cylinder $2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z$, sphere $3\varepsilon$. Rule of thumb: **whatever the exponent of a length in the volume formula is, that is the multiplier on its strain** ($\pi R^2L$ → $R$ gets 2; $R^3$ → gets 3).

**Thermal expansion, volume version** | Front: for uniform heating $\Delta T$ with no constraint, what is the fractional volume change of the vessel? Does it produce stress? | Back: $3\alpha\Delta T$ (each direction gets $\alpha\Delta T$, three directions); it produces no stress - free expansion has no internal force, and thermal stress needs a temperature gradient or a constraint.

**3D Hooke + heat** | Front: write the full expression for the hoop strain. | Back: $\varepsilon_\theta=\dfrac{\sigma_\theta-\nu(\sigma_z+\sigma_r)}{E}+\alpha\Delta T$; for a thin wall take $\sigma_r\approx0$ to get $\dfrac{\sigma_\theta-\nu\sigma_z}{E}+\alpha\Delta T$. The Poisson term is minus $\nu\times$(sum of the other two stresses), and the thermal term is added directly.

**The answers to this problem (quick reference)** | Front: what are the final numbers for the two parts of Q4.2? | Back: (a) cylinder $\Delta V=0.101\ \text{m}^3\approx101\ \text{L}$ ($\Delta V_p=5.77\times10^{-2}$, $\Delta V_T=4.34\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}^3$); (b) spherical tank $\Delta V=0.100\ \text{m}^3\approx100\ \text{L}$ ($4.25\times10^{-2}+5.78\times10^{-2}$).
