## 1. Problem summary

**Given**

| Item | Symbol | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Material | — | Aluminium |
| Young's modulus | $E$ | $69\ \text{GPa}=69\,000\ \text{MPa}$ |
| Poisson's ratio | $\nu$ | $0.3$ |
| Coefficient of linear expansion | $\alpha$ | $23\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ |
| Mean radius | $R_m$ (written $R$) | $2\ \text{m}$ |
| Cylinder length | $L$ | $2\ \text{m}$ |
| Wall thickness | $t$ | $120\ \text{mm}=0.12\ \text{m}$ |
| Internal pressure (gauge) | $p$ | $5\ \text{MPa}$ |
| Temperature rise (uniform, unconstrained) | $\Delta T$ | $+25\ \text{K}$ |

**Find**

- **(a)** The total change of the **internal volume** $\Delta V_{\text{cyl}}$ of the closed-ended thin-walled cylinder under internal pressure + temperature rise;
- **(b)** $\Delta V_{\text{sph}}$ for a thin-walled **spherical tank** of the same mean radius and wall thickness under the same conditions.

**Key assumptions** (details in core §3): thin-walled membrane ($R/t=16.7$, $\sigma_r$ neglected), closed ends ($\sigma_z\neq0$), small-deformation linear elasticity (superposition valid), uniform temperature rise (no thermal stress), no external constraint, $p$ is gauge pressure. **What is wanted is the internal volume** ($\pi R^2L$, $\tfrac43\pi R^3$), not the wall material volume - the wall-thickness strain $\varepsilon_r$ does not enter the result.

## 2. Core solution (tabulated, with units)

### 2.1 Chain of formulas

| Stage | Cylinder | Sphere |
|---|---|---|
| Membrane stress | $\sigma_\theta=\dfrac{pR}{t}$, $\sigma_z=\dfrac{pR}{2t}=\dfrac{\sigma_\theta}{2}$ | $\sigma=\sigma_\phi=\sigma_\theta=\dfrac{pR}{2t}$ |
| Pressure strain | $\varepsilon_\theta^{p}=\dfrac{\sigma_\theta-\nu\sigma_z}{E}$, $\varepsilon_z^{p}=\dfrac{\sigma_z-\nu\sigma_\theta}{E}$ | $\varepsilon^{p}=\dfrac{\sigma(1-\nu)}{E}$ |
| Thermal strain (each direction) | $+\alpha\Delta T$ (no stress, free expansion) | As above |
| Volumetric strain | $\varepsilon_v=2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z$ | $\varepsilon_v=3\varepsilon$ |
| Volume change | $\Delta V=V\,(\varepsilon_v^{p}+3\alpha\Delta T)$ | As above ($V=\tfrac43\pi R^3$) |
| Cylinder shortcut | $\varepsilon_v^{p}=\dfrac{pR}{tE}\left(\dfrac52-2\nu\right)$ | $\varepsilon_v^{p}=\dfrac{3(1-\nu)pR}{2tE}$ |

### 2.2 Numerical results (3 significant figures, units throughout; all 18 items independently recomputed by t3 and consistent)

| Quantity | Cylinder (a) | Sphere (b) |
|---|---|---|
| Internal volume $V$ | $25.1\ \text{m}^3$ | $33.5\ \text{m}^3$ |
| Hoop stress $\sigma_\theta$ | $83.3\ \text{MPa}$ | $41.7\ \text{MPa}$ |
| Axial / meridional stress $\sigma_z\,(=\sigma_\phi)$ | $41.7\ \text{MPa}$ | $41.7\ \text{MPa}$ |
| Hoop pressure strain $\varepsilon_\theta^{p}$ | $1.03\times10^{-3}$ | $4.23\times10^{-4}$ |
| Axial pressure strain $\varepsilon_z^{p}$ | $2.42\times10^{-4}$ | $4.23\times10^{-4}$ |
| Pressure volumetric strain $\varepsilon_v^{p}$ | $2.29\times10^{-3}$ | $1.27\times10^{-3}$ |
| Thermal volumetric strain $3\alpha\Delta T$ | $1.73\times10^{-3}$ | $1.73\times10^{-3}$ |

| Dimensionless $\Delta V/V$ | $4.02\times10^{-3}$ | $3.00\times10^{-3}$ |

**Why the two parts come out almost level**: the cylinder has the higher stress ($\sigma_\theta$ is twice that of the sphere) but the smaller volume, while the spherical tank has the lower stress but the larger volume - the pressure term is 36% larger for the cylinder, the temperature term is 33% larger for the sphere, and adding them brings them level. Neither term can be dropped.

**Thin wall vs thick wall (comparison only, not the answer)**: the Lamé solution with $R_i=1.94$, $R_o=2.06$ gives $\Delta V=0.096\ \text{m}^3$ for the cylinder, $4.7\%$ lower than the thin-wall answer - the error comes mainly from the reference volume (bore volume $23.65$ vs $25.13\ \text{m}^3$, $-5.9\%$), not from the stress. The question prescribes $R_m$, so the thin-wall answer is the one it wants.

## 3. Walkthrough (aimed at second-year engineering students)

### The one-line intuition

Two things stretch the tank at once: **the internal pressure pushes from inside** (stress → strain), and **heating makes the material itself longer** (free thermal strain, which produces no stress). Volume goes as the cube of a length, so $\Delta V\approx\varepsilon_v\times V$; for the cylinder $\varepsilon_v=2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z$ (the radius counts twice), for the sphere $\varepsilon_v=3\varepsilon$. The pressure term favours the cylinder, the temperature term favours the sphere, and they offset - $101\ \text{L}$ against $100\ \text{L}$.

### Step-by-step (for each step: what is computed / why this way)

**Step 0 Be clear first about what is being computed**
The change of the **internal volume**, not the wall volume; and because the deformation is small we do not compute "new size − old size" but a ratio $\Delta V/V=\varepsilon_v$ which is then multiplied back - that is exactly what the total-differential step does.

**Step 1 Compute the original volume $V$**
$V=\pi\times2^2\times2=25.133\ \text{m}^3$, sphere $33.510\ \text{m}^3$. Why first: the final $\Delta V=V\cdot\varepsilon_v$ multiplies by it, and it is the anchor for the magnitude check (the answer must be of order $V\times10^{-3}$).

**Step 2 Compute the stresses (force balance, no $E$)**
Cut a free body: hoop $\sigma_\theta=pR/t=83.33\ \text{MPa}$; axial, with the end caps passing the force, $\sigma_z=pR/2t=41.67\ \text{MPa}$; sphere sharing equally in two directions, $\sigma=pR/2t=41.67\ \text{MPa}$. Equilibrium only asks where the force comes from and goes to, independent of the material stiffness, so $E$ only appears in the next step. The denominator for the sphere is $2t$ - two directions sharing equally; the first big place to lose marks.

**Step 3 Compute the strains (Hooke + Poisson + heat)**
The three normal stresses pull on each other: $\varepsilon_\theta=(\sigma_\theta-\nu\sigma_z)/E$, $\varepsilon_z=(\sigma_z-\nu\sigma_\theta)/E$, sphere $\sigma(1-\nu)/E$, then add $\alpha\Delta T$ to all. Why you cannot just divide $\sigma/E$ and stop: the Poisson term squeezes $\varepsilon_z$ from $6.04\times10^{-4}$ down to $2.415\times10^{-4}$ (about 1/4). The thermal strain produces no stress - uniform heating with no constraint means free expansion.

**Step 4 Linear strain → volumetric strain (the key move)**
The radius is squared in $V=\pi R^2L$: $\Delta V/V=2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z$; the sphere is a cube: $3\varepsilon$. Rule of thumb: **whatever the exponent of a length in the volume formula is, that is the multiplier on its strain**. Check: pure thermal expansion gives $3\alpha\Delta T=1.725\times10^{-3}$ by both routes.

**Step 5 Compute the pressure term and the temperature term separately, then add**
$\Delta V=\Delta V_p+\Delta V_T=V\,(\varepsilon_v^{p}+3\alpha\Delta T)$. Adding is safe because linear elastic + small deformation → superposition, and the temperature term carries no stress so it does not contaminate the pressure term.

**Step 6 Do the sphere the same way, then look back and compare**
Three substitutions: stress $pR/2t$, strain $\sigma(1-\nu)/E$, volume $3\varepsilon$. This gives $\Delta V_{\text{sph}}=0.100\ \text{m}^3$.

**The 30-second exam flow**:
$$
V \;\to\; \sigma\ (pR/t,\ pR/2t) \;\to\; \varepsilon\ (\text{with Poisson}+\alpha\Delta T) \;\to\; \varepsilon_v\ (2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z\ /\ 3\varepsilon) \;\to\; \Delta V_p+\Delta V_T
$$

### Common mistakes (many people ... whereas in fact ...)

1. **Many people** write the cylinder volumetric strain as $\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z$; **in fact** the radius is squared in $\pi R^2L$ - it is $2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z$, and dropping the 2 makes the pressure term a third too small.
2. **Many people** think heating creates a thermal stress that stretches the tank further; **in fact** with uniform heating and no constraint the thermal strain carries no stress, and the temperature term is simply $3\alpha\Delta T$; a thermal stress needs a temperature gradient or a constraint.
3. **Many people** memorise the sphere stress as $pR/t$; **in fact** for a sphere it is $pR/(2t)$ - two directions sharing equally; make that mistake and the sphere strain and $\Delta V$ are both out by a factor of two.
4. **Many people** write $\varepsilon_z=\sigma_z/E$; **in fact** the Poisson term must be there, $(\sigma_z-\nu\sigma_\theta)/E$ - the hoop tension pinches the axial direction back.
5. **Many people** think "thin wall, neglect $\sigma_r$" means the radial direction can be ignored completely; **in fact** only the radial stress is neglected; $\varepsilon_r$ is still produced by Poisson - it only changes the wall thickness, so it does not enter the internal-volume formula.
6. **Many people** carry mixed units ($t=120$, $E=69$); **in fact** go either fully SI or fully MPa - consistent at both ends, otherwise you are out by six orders of magnitude.

## 5. Memory cards

**Thin-cylinder stress** | Front: what are the hoop and axial stresses in a closed thin-walled cylinder, and why do they differ by a factor of two? | Back: $\sigma_\theta=pR/t$, $\sigma_z=pR/2t=\sigma_\theta/2$. The hoop direction carries the whole projected pressure on its own, while the axial direction has the end caps passing the force but a larger wall ring area - cut a free body and it falls out, no need to memorise it.

**Sphere stress** | Front: what is the stress formula for a thin spherical shell? | Back: $\sigma=\sigma_\phi=\sigma_\theta=\dfrac{pR}{2t}$, the two principal stresses are equal. The denominator is $2t$ - spherical symmetry, two directions sharing equally; this is the only, but fatal, difference from the cylinder.

**Volumetric strain** | Front: how do you write $\Delta V/V$ for a cylinder and a sphere in terms of strain? | Back: cylinder $2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z$, sphere $3\varepsilon$. Rule of thumb: **whatever the exponent of a length in the volume formula is, that is the multiplier on its strain**.

**Thermal expansion, volume version** | Front: for uniform heating $\Delta T$ with no constraint, what is the fractional volume change of the vessel? Does it produce stress? | Back: $3\alpha\Delta T$ (each direction gets $\alpha\Delta T$); it produces no stress - free expansion has no internal force, and thermal stress needs a temperature gradient or a constraint.

**3D Hooke + heat** | Front: write the full expression for the hoop strain. | Back: $\varepsilon_\theta=\dfrac{\sigma_\theta-\nu(\sigma_z+\sigma_r)}{E}+\alpha\Delta T$; for a thin wall take $\sigma_r\approx0$ to get $\dfrac{\sigma_\theta-\nu\sigma_z}{E}+\alpha\Delta T$. The Poisson term is minus $\nu\times$(sum of the other two stresses), and the thermal term is added directly.

**The answers to this problem (quick reference)** | Front: what are the final numbers for the two parts of Q4.2? | Back: (a) cylinder $\Delta V=0.101\ \text{m}^3\approx101\ \text{L}$ ($\Delta V_p=5.77\times10^{-2}$, $\Delta V_T=4.34\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}^3$); (b) spherical tank $\Delta V=0.100\ \text{m}^3\approx100\ \text{L}$ ($4.25\times10^{-2}+5.78\times10^{-2}$).

## 6. One-line summary

**How much more a tank holds when it is both pressurised and heated equals "volumetric strain × original volume" - the cylinder uses $2\varepsilon_\theta+\varepsilon_z$, the sphere uses $3\varepsilon$, and the temperature term always adds $3\alpha\Delta T$; the answers here are $0.101\ \text{m}^3$ (cylinder) and $0.100\ \text{m}^3$ (spherical tank), all 18 independent recomputations passed, and the two tanks come out almost level because the cylinder has the higher stress with the smaller volume while the sphere has the lower stress with the larger volume.**
