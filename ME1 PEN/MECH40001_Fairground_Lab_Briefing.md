## 1. The nature of an experiment: ask three questions first (10-19)

The lecture breaks every experiment down into a fixed skeleton. Before you touch anything you must be able to answer:

1. **What is the aim** — what you want to measure and what you want to show (the "step one" before 12:06)
2. **What do I want to measure** — which physical quantity exactly
3. **How are we going to measure that** — by what method
4. **What equipment will you require** — what equipment you need

The lecturer's example is homely but clear: to measure the length of a kettle you first settle "the thing I am measuring is length", then "I measure it with a ruler", and finally "a straight ruler or a tape measure". The order of the three steps cannot be reversed (10:41–11:46).

Having a hypothesis is better, but it is not required — the experiment itself may bring out something interesting (09:31–10:05).

## 2. Safety and risk assessment (10-19, 12:06–12:54)

A **risk assessment** must be done before the experiment; it is not a formality:

- you can hurt yourself, hurt someone else, or damage equipment
- identify the risk → assess it → mitigate it, to push the chance of an incident as low as possible
- if something does go wrong, have a response ready (for example what to do about a small fire)

## 3. Logbook: what this lab is really marked on (10-19)

### 3.1 What it is and why it exists

- It is the **green book** handed out at enrolment (the one in the burgundy bag), called log book or logbook interchangeably in the lectures (17:29–18:03)
- The name comes from seafaring: a wooden log on a knotted rope was thrown into the water, the knots were counted to get the ship's speed, and the result was "written into the log" (19:22–20:16)
- **Step zero: take it to the lab.** In the lecturer's words, at least two students did not bring theirs last Friday (18:15–18:27)

### 3.2 The question that gets asked most often (16:11–16:38)

> "What speed was used for the tensile test?"

The lecturer's answer is always: "**the value you wrote in your logbook.**" And the conversation ends there.

That anecdote is the whole lesson of this lab: **write it down on the spot, do not count on remembering it afterwards**.

### 3.3 What to record and how

- **Record everything**, including what seems unimportant at the time (21:11–21:26)
- A real case: a colleague was doing a carbon-fibre single-filament tensile test at the weekend; the lecturer left and closed the door, and a small dip appeared in the curve that matched the moment of the door closing. She recorded that "irrelevant" detail, and it was the only way the data made sense later (21:35–22:49)
- **Never tear out pages** (23:10–25:05). The reason is not "wasting paper" but this: a missing page means you are hiding something, which means your data cannot be trusted
- **Write in pen, not pencil** (25:05–25:47): pencil rubs off, and writing in pen also shows you are confident in your record
- If you make a mistake, **cross it out**; do not overwrite it and do not delete it (29:24–29:37)
- **It only has to be legible, not neat** (26:02). But **at least one person must be able to read it** — that person is first of all you, and second, because it is marked, the marker (26:33–26:55)

### 3.4 The entries a logbook should contain (30:59–32:32)

In the order the lecturer gives:

1. **Date** — the day the experiment was actually done
2. **Title** — give the experiment a name
3. **Aim** — what the experiment is meant to do
4. **Apparatus and set-up** — describe the equipment, ideally with a **sketch** to help you remember
5. **Method** — what you actually did (someone else must be able to repeat it)
6. **Raw data** — copy the data down as it comes out
7. **Thoughts at the time** — whatever crossed your mind that you thought might matter
8. **Rough graphs and calculations** — a hand-drawn curve, a quick calculation
9. **Summary** — "the experiment went roughly to plan / every sample broke, it has to be repeated"

### 3.5 When it is assessed (27:09–27:26)

> The first-year green logbook **is assessed after the fairground lab**.

In other words: **the Fairground Lab is the assessment point for this logbook**.
Later labs are not assessed with it, but you still take it to them — because you still need the record.

## 4. Precision vs accuracy (10-19, 38:19–39:56)

| | Precise | Not precise |
|---|---|---|
| **Accurate** | the ideal: tight to the true value, small error | the mean is right but the scatter is large |
| **Not accurate** | clustered, but systematically off | the worst: off and scattered |

- **Accuracy** means close to the true value
- **Precision** means small uncertainty
- The two are independent: being precise does not mean being accurate
- The four dartboard pictures are simply the visual version of these four cells

In the lab, every time you measure a value, ask: is this number accurate? Is it precise enough?

## 5. How to write the Fairground Report (10-12)

- This report is marked on **communication**, not on "how hard an experiment you did" (07:26–07:33)
- **The reader is the Health and Safety Executive (HSE)** (30:23–30:27).
  Before every sentence, ask: would someone at the HSE understand this, and is it useful to them?
- Structure: **take the reader by the hand** all the way to your conclusion; put the information in a logical order, do not jump about (09:56, 20:08)
- **Do not vary your words for the sake of variety**: always use the same word for the same thing. What is marked is clarity, not your stock of synonyms (19:17)
- **Make a report plan before you write**: writing without a plan takes longer to fix afterwards (46:43–46:54)
- The report must be **concise** — do not ramble or waffle (11-02, 05:46, 08:27)

## 7. Three things to remember

1. **Take the green book, write on the spot, write the units.** The tensile-speed question is the answer.
2. **No torn pages, no overwriting, just cross it out.** A missing page means questionable data.
3. **The report reader is the HSE, and the outline comes before the writing.** What is marked is communication, not showing off.
